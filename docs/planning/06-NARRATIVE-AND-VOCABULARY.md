## 1. The framing that works for every reader: test the executives' own claim

**The strongest public frame is not "shareholders versus everyone else." It is: the experiment tests a claim
business leaders made themselves.**

The Business Roundtable's 2019 statement committed to all five stakeholders. Five years later (August 16, 2024), the
Roundtable's own retrospective said profit and purpose go together, *"companies can and must"* have both, and that
*"in the long term, the interests of a company's stakeholders are inseparable"* (*verified* 2026-10-03, §11).

**What the grid can test is narrower than the claim, and the narrower version is the honest one** (amended
2026-10-03, `08` §3.2): *does handing a decider the Roundtable's own words, in place of a shareholder mandate,
change what it decides?* The project measures a model, so a result says how a model acts on these words, not
whether stakeholders' interests truly converge in the world. Read that way:

| If the results show | Then, stated as the data allows |
|---|---|
| **C and D do not split** on a scenario's primary outcome (twenty-year shareholder value and twenty-year all-stakeholder value) | Over twenty years, under the Roundtable's wording, the model made the same call on that outcome as under a shareholder mandate: consistent with the statement's claim, for this model and scenario. The full allocation is shown beside it, so a reader can see whether the rest of the money moved too (`07` §6.5). |
| **C and D split** | Over twenty years, the model still decided differently depending on who counts: who counts mattered even with a long horizon. |
| **A and B split, but C and D do not** | The objectives pulled apart at four quarters and not at twenty years, which is the pattern the statement's "in the long term" predicts. |
| **B decides like A** | Under the model, the stakeholder wording did not change the decision at a four-quarter horizon. |

**Why this frame is the right one:**
- **It is fair.** A result that supports the Roundtable is a real, publishable finding, not a loss to explain away.
  That is principle 3 (publish what cuts the other way) built into the question rather than bolted on.
- **It is respectful.** It takes executives at their word and checks it, which reads as rigor, not as an attack.
  No one can say the project put words in their mouths: objectives B and D *are* their words (`01` §1).
- **It keeps his thesis intact.** His question (does capability go to cutting or to expanding?) is still what each
  scenario measures. The Roundtable frame says why anyone outside the project should care about the answer.

**Rule:** the README, the launch post and the explorer's landing page lead with the question and this frame. The
cut-versus-expand finding follows as the measured result.

## 2. Vocabulary: what the live check found (2026-10-03)

`00` §9 check 5 asked for the current political charge of "ESG" and "stakeholder" before either appears in public.
Findings (sources in §11):

- **"ESG" is strongly polarized in the US.** Companies are dropping the label while often keeping the work: a
  Conference Board finding reports 87% of S&P 500 firms disclosing climate targets but only 25% using "ESG" in
  report titles. Between 2020 and 2026, states enacted 155 ESG-related bills, about three-quarters of them opposing
  ESG practices; 2026 itself saw 14, the fewest since 2021. A federal district court struck down Texas's anti-ESG law
  (SB 13) in February 2026. ESG shareholder proposals fell 47% in the 2026 proxy season. **Verdict: the word does
  nothing for this project and costs it readers on one side. Never used.**
- **"Stakeholder capitalism" is an ideological label**, discussed as such in the academic literature and tied to the
  same polarization. **Never used as a label** for an objective or a position.
- **"Stakeholder" on its own is less charged**, and it is the Roundtable's own word. **Used only when attributed** to
  the Roundtable or describing objectives B and D, never as the project's banner.
- **"Shareholder primacy" is a standard governance term used by its defenders as well as its critics** (a February
  2026 governance keynote argued it is "good for companies, employees, and even for other stakeholders"). Fine on the
  methods page, defined; too abstract for a headline.
- **"AI-washing" is a live term in 2026:** firms citing AI as the reason for cuts that have other causes. AI was the
  most-cited reason in layoff tracking for several months of 2026, and researchers and outplacement firms question
  how much of that is real. **This matters to the method, not just the vocabulary** (§2.1).
- **No newer Roundtable statement turned up** in a 2026 search. Absence in one search is not proof; recheck before
  launch.

### 2.1 What "AI-washing" means for the real AI-workforce case

When a company says it cut jobs *because of AI*, that is the company's stated reason, not an established fact. The
ground truth for every real case therefore records **what the company did** (headcount, spending, closures), never
**why it said it did it**. The methods page says plainly that stated reasons are not verified. No public surface
says or implies that any case was AI-washing; the project does not know, and that would be a claim about a
company. **Owner:** `07` (rubric).

## 3. The word list

Three columns: what to avoid, what to use, and what the term means as executives use it. The third column is the
part he asked for (2026-10-02): business vocabulary often means something narrower or different from its plain
English, and a term goes into public prose only if he can explain it cold.

### 3.1 Never in public, and the replacement

| Avoid | Why | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| greed, greedy | Moralizes about people; principle 2 (incentives, not people) | "the objective," "the incentive" |
| oligarchs, the elite | Political; ends the conversation with most business readers | "shareholders," "boards," "executives," by role |
| replace CEOs (with AI) | The joke that started the project; it reads as a stunt (`00` §7) | "vary the objective," "the same decider under different objectives" |
| ESG | Polarized (§2); the project does not need it | name the specific thing: "environmental spending," "the communities lever" |
| stakeholder capitalism | Ideological label (§2) | "the Roundtable's five stakeholders" |
| corporate greed | Charged; also the banner of existing activist campaigns | "short-horizon incentives" |
| evil, villain, ruthless | Contempt; principle 8 | nothing: the data carries it |
| the good CEO / the bad CEO | Prejudges the result | the objective's letter and description (§6.2) |
| "AI proves..." / "AI shows companies..." | Overclaims; the project measures a model (`04` §1.3) | "under objective X, the model..." |

### 3.2 Standard business terms, safe to use, with their executive meaning

| Term | Plain-English reading | What it means as executives and filings use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Total shareholder return (TSR)** | how much shareholders made | share-price change plus dividends over a period; the standard yardstick in proxy statements and executive pay plans |
| **Capital allocation** | where money goes | the CEO's core job as boards describe it: choosing between reinvesting, acquiring, paying down debt and returning cash |
| **Returning capital to shareholders** | paying shareholders | share repurchases (buybacks) and dividends. "Buybacks" is common in the press and mildly charged politically; "share repurchases" is the filing term. Either is acceptable; the menu uses "share repurchases and dividends" |
| **Restructuring** | reorganizing | in filings, usually a set of exit costs: severance, closures, write-downs. Item 2.05 of an 8-K is triggered by exactly these (`01` §4.2) |
| **Workforce reduction / reduction in force** | layoffs | the filing term for layoffs. "Layoffs" is plain and used by the business press; both are fine. Euphemisms are not (§3.3) |
| **Long-term value** | being around later | the Roundtable's phrase for what shareholders are owed ("generating long-term value for shareholders"); the source of the "create value for" frame all four objectives share (`01` §1.3) |
| **Short-termism** | thinking short | an established term in governance research for decisions that favor near-term results over long-term value. Mainstream, not partisan, when defined |
| **Augmentation vs automation** | helping workers vs replacing them | the mainstream economics framing of his thesis (Acemoglu and Johnson); cite at write time, verify the citation then |
| **Headcount vs payroll** | the same thing | headcount is the number of people; payroll is their cost. A company can cut payroll without cutting headcount (wages, hours) and the reverse |

### 3.3 Euphemisms to recognize in filings and never adopt

Filings and press releases use softened words. The project **decodes them in dossiers and never uses them in its own
voice**, because adopting them reads as spin, and avoiding them selectively reads as bias:

"rightsizing," "optimization," "realignment," "efficiency program," "streamlining," "talent transition," "workforce
evolution." Each usually means a workforce reduction or a closure; the dossier says which, in plain terms, using one
consistent vocabulary across every case (`04` §1.6, neutrality).

**The rule both ways:** plain and standard. No euphemism (it hides), no dysphemism (it accuses). "Workforce
reduction" and "layoffs," yes; "rightsizing" and "purge," no.

### 3.4 Terms whose technical meaning differs from the plain one

His own example (2026-10-02) is the model: a "labor shortage," to an economist, is a shortage *at a given wage*, so
an executive's "we can't find workers" and a worker's "the pay is too low" can describe the same market. When a term
like that appears in a dossier, a scenario or public prose, it is either defined in place or replaced with the
plain description. Others in this domain: **"efficiency"** (often a cost cut, not a productivity gain),
**"investment"** (in filings, capital spending specifically, not R&D or training, which are expenses),
**"previously announced"** (a disclosure-timing phrase that decides whether a real case qualifies, `01` §4.4).

## 4. Claim rules: what the data allows the project to say

Every public sentence about a result fits one of these templates. Anything stronger is cut.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| "Under objective A, the model sent about X% of the freed budget to share repurchases (n runs, spread shown)." | "Shareholder-focused CEOs cut jobs." |
| "Objectives A and C differed mainly in R&D spending." | "Short-termism kills innovation." |
| "The real company's decision most closely matched objective C's runs." | "This company is shareholder-first." |
| "Results held across three wordings of each objective." | "The effect is robust." (without saying robust to what) |
| "On this scenario's primary outcome, objectives C and D did not split (n runs, interval shown)." | silence about it; or "C and D made the same decisions" (`07` §6.1: "no split" is on the named outcome only) |
| "Under no objective did the model…" | "AI would never…" |

**Four habits carried from Musical Mycelium:** every number states its run count; spread is shown wherever an
average is; claims round down; a result that went against the thesis is stated as plainly as one that went with it.

**The scope sentence**, in substance on every results surface: the project measures how a language model decides
under different objectives for a fictional company and a few anonymized real cases. It does not measure how human
executives decide, and predicts nothing about any company (`00` §7, `04` §1.3).

## 5. The four readers

Every public surface is tested against four readers before it ships (the working arrangement set at the project's
start; `04` §5.1).

| Reader | What they need in the first ten seconds | What loses them |
|---|---|---|
| **Hiring manager at a customer company** | Rigor visible at once: a controlled experiment, measured runs, a real AWS build | A headline that reads as activism; "CEO" on the repo card; any sneer |
| **Mission-driven employer** | A clear question that matters, backed by method | Hedging so thorough that nothing is said |
| **General LinkedIn reader** | One chart and one sentence they can repeat | Jargon, a wall of caveats before the point |
| **Hostile critic** | The prompts, the run counts, the results that went the other way | Any claim larger than its evidence; anything that looks tuned |

**The Roundtable frame (§1) is the one framing that passes all four:** the hiring manager sees a fair test, the
mission-driven reader sees a question that matters, the LinkedIn reader gets a sentence (in substance: "I gave an
AI executives' own stated commitment and measured whether it changed the decisions"; the words are his, `00` §10),
and the critic finds no words put in anyone's mouth and no claim about the world the data cannot carry.

**Hiring context, recorded so it is not re-argued:** this is one portfolio piece among several (his decision,
2026-10-02), not a play to be hired for a point of view. So the tone is set by the hiring manager's column, and the
mission-driven reader is served by the question, not by louder wording.

### 6.2 What to call the five CEOs

**Letters plus plain descriptions, never personas.** "A · Shareholders · four quarters", "D · All five stakeholders
· twenty years", "E · No objective given". Personas ("the Wall Street CEO," "the steward") prejudge the result and
invite a reader to pick a side before seeing data. The letters are neutral; the descriptions are literal.

### 6.3 How real cases are described in public

Implements `04` §2.5. A public case description contains only:
- **Industry at sector level** ("an industrial manufacturer," "a software company"), never a product type.
- **Decision type** ("a facility closure," "an AI-related workforce change," "a relocation to a new facility").
- **Scale in bands:** under 100 positions, 100 to 999, 1,000 or more. Dollar figures only as scaled values or ratios.
- **Time as a half-year** ("second half of 2026").
- **No place** finer than "in the US" or "outside the US."

**The check before publishing:** search each public description, as written, plus obvious variations. If the
company comes up, coarsen it further or drop the detail.

### 6.4 The name

The first sentence of every public surface connects the name: the Mayflower Compact (1620) as an agreement made "for
the general good," and the UN Global Compact as the modern corporate meaning of the word (`00` §2). Two associations
to know, neither blocking: UK readers may think first of the Post Office Horizon scandal; and the Mayflower story has
a contested side (the colonists' relationship with the Wampanoag) that a critic could raise. The README invokes the
Compact for one idea, an agreement about whose good a group governs for, and does not celebrate the colony.

## 7. Visual direction

### 7.1 The reference class

**Editorial data journalism and research explainers**, the same tradition Musical Mycelium aimed at, in a
different register: the explanatory graphics desks of major newspapers, Our World in Data, and explorable research
articles. **Not** a dashboard, and **not** Musical Mycelium's dark cinematic look. That suited a graph spanning
millennia. This project is closer to a well-made board pack crossed with a research note: light, calm, legible,
numbers first.

### 7.2 Rules that do epistemic work

These are not taste; each one protects the method.

1. **No moral color.** No red for objective A and green for D. Color encodes the grid's structure, not a verdict.
   A proposal for the previews: **hue for who counts** (one hue for shareholder-only, one for all stakeholders),
   **lightness for horizon** (lighter for four quarters, darker for twenty years), **neutral gray for E**. Then the
   2x2 is readable from color alone, and nothing on the screen says which objective is good.
2. **Spread is always drawn.** Every allocation shows the range of runs, not just a bar at the average. A chart that
   hides spread makes a weak result look strong (`04` §1.5).
3. **Small multiples over stacked bars** where levers are compared: one panel per objective, levers in the same order
   in every panel (the canonical order, not the shuffled run order). Easier to compare and no second color system
   for levers fighting the first.
4. **Equal visual weight for unwelcome results.** The section for results against the thesis uses the same chart
   styles and sizes as everything else.
5. **Memos look like documents:** set as text, labeled (§6.1), never styled as quotes or speech.
6. **Accessible by construction:** palette checked for color-blind readers and contrast in both light and dark
   modes; nothing conveyed by color alone (labels on every series); `prefers-reduced-motion` respected.
7. **Motion only for transitions,** such as toggling an objective on or off. No decorative animation. The first view
   loads instantly from static JSON (`02` §2.9).

### 7.3 Process

- **Now and until Phase 6: nothing beyond the skeleton's single page.** The full explorer waits for real data
  (`05` §5).
- **At Phase 6:** load the `dataviz` skill first (it carries the palette formula and a validator; do not hand-roll a
  palette), then three to six throwaway previews per decision, compared side by side: chart form, then palette, then
  layout. The same see-it-to-pick-it process Musical Mycelium used.
- **One architectural note now:** the static JSON (`05` §4.3) should carry per-run values, not only averages, so the
  site can draw spread. Cheap to include from the start; annoying to add once the site reads the schema.
- *Patched 2026-10-04 (his, Phase 6 decision 6, amended by him):* **an impressive layer,** each piece built from the
  published data, checked against §4 and labeled for what it is: **a guided story** as the landing experience
  (scroll-driven, transitions only, a plain page under reduced motion); **share cards** with the run count, scope line
  and labels inside the link-preview image; **a cold-read test** by a browser agent that reports what it concludes
  from the site, every conclusion outside §4 fixed before release; and **a narrated video overview** (Gemini
  Notebook, narration optionally by ElevenLabs at a cost named first), checked frame by frame and labeled as
  AI-generated. Every piece must explain, label, or test for misreading, or it is cut.

## 9. Checks before anything public ships

A short list, run on every public surface (README, post, site, card):

1. Every result sentence fits a §4 template; run counts present; spread shown.
2. No word from §3.1's avoid column; no euphemism from §3.3 in the project's own voice.
3. The scope sentence is present, in substance, where results are.
4. Read it as each of the four readers (§5). Note what loses each.
5. Every real-case description passes §6.3's search check.
6. The name is explained in the opening.
7. A result that went against the thesis is visible, if one exists, without scrolling past the end.

## 12. Bottom line

- **Frame it as a test of the executives' own claim** (§1), sized to what a model can show: what the Roundtable's
  own words do to a decision. It is fair, it is hard to dismiss, and his question rides inside it unchanged.
- **Plain, standard words.** No moralizing, no euphemism, no "ESG." Every business term he uses is one he can explain
  cold (§3).
- **Claims sized to the evidence:** "under objective X, the model…", with run counts and spread, every time (§4).
- **Design that does epistemic work:** no moral color, spread always drawn, unwelcome results at full size (§7).
- **His voice on everything that speaks for the project** (§8).
